    The Missing Fundraiser Money

    $3,100 of a youth soccer league’s money is gone. You’ll act as judge, then juror, in a criminal trial and a civil trial. This time the evidence has holes, the witnesses don’t always agree, and some evidence is allowed in one courtroom but not the other.

    What happened

    On Saturday, April 11, the Harbor Youth Soccer League held a car wash and bake sale to raise money for new uniforms. When it ended, two volunteers counted the cash box and wrote the total on a count sheet.

    League treasurer Jordan Price, age 34, took the cash box home to deposit at the bank on Monday. The league’s rule book says two people must always handle cash together, but Jordan took the box alone. The rule book also lets the treasurer keep a $500 change float (small bills for making change) for the next event.

    On Monday, Jordan deposited $1,600. The league board did the math and called the police. Jordan says someone must have taken money from the box while his car was parked at a diner Saturday night.

    Two cases, two different jobs

    Real trials don’t ask “Did this person do something bad?” Each charge or claim is broken into parts called elements. The side bringing the case has to prove every element.

    Criminal case

    The State v. Jordan Price

    Charge: Embezzlement, which means stealing money you were trusted to take care of.

    The prosecutor must prove all three elements beyond a reasonable doubt:

    1. Jordan was trusted with the league’s money.
    2. Jordan took some of it for himself.
    3. He did it on purpose, not by accident or mistake.

    Possible sentence: up to 12 months in jail, a fine, and paying the money back.

    Civil case

    Harbor Youth Soccer League v. Price

    The league wins if it proves either claim, more likely than not:

    Claim 1, taking: Jordan took the league’s money for himself.

    Claim 2, carelessness: Jordan had a duty to protect the money, he broke that duty, and that caused money to be lost.

    Possible result: Jordan pays the league back for what was lost.

    Notice something? The civil case has a second way to win. The league might not be able to prove Jordan stole anything, but it might prove he was careless. Keep that in mind.

    Before the criminal trial: you’re the judge

    Before a jury hears anything, lawyers argue about what evidence is allowed. These arguments are called objections. Decide each one, then read how a real judge would rule.

    Whatever you decided, the trial will follow the real rules. The jury will not see the envelope or hear Coach Ito’s secondhand story, and the prosecutor can’t comment on Jordan’s silence.

    Criminal trial

    The State v. Jordan Price

    Jordan has chosen not to testify, which is his right under the Fifth Amendment. Read each exhibit and its cross-examination. Then answer the question: what does this evidence actually prove?

    Think like a strong juror. Separate what a piece of evidence shows directly from what someone wants you to guess from it. Ask who is speaking, how they know, and whether they have a reason to shade the truth.

    Criminal trial

    Jury room: the criminal verdict

    The judge explains: “You must decide each element separately. If even one element is not proven beyond a reasonable doubt, your verdict must be not guilty. Jordan’s choice not to testify cannot be held against him.”

      Civil trial

      Harbor Youth Soccer League v. Price

      The league sues Jordan to get its money back. You’re on a new jury. Exhibits 1 through 9 come in again, but three important things change.

      1. The envelope is allowed

      The illegal search was thrown out of the criminal trial. See Exhibit 10 for why the civil jury gets to see it.

      2. Jordan has to testify

      In a civil case, the other side can call the defendant as a witness and question him under oath.

      3. The bar is lower, and there are two claims

      The league only has to show its claims are more likely true than not. And it can win by proving Jordan was careless, even if it can’t prove he stole.

      Coach Ito’s secondhand story is still not allowed. The rule against hearsay applies in both kinds of court.

      Jordan’s testimony

      The league’s lawyer, Ms. Okafor, questions Jordan under oath.

      Ms. Okafor: Did you lock your car at the diner?
      Jordan: “Yes. I always lock my car.”
      Ms. Okafor: Why didn’t you count the cash Saturday night?
      Jordan: “I was exhausted. I counted it Monday morning, and there was only $2,100.”
      Ms. Okafor: Where did the $400 envelope come from?
      Jordan: “That was left over from last fall’s raffle. I forgot to turn it in.”
      Ms. Okafor: When did you sell your motorcycle?
      Jordan: “A couple of weeks before the fundraiser. I kept the cash in a drawer at home and finally deposited it Tuesday.”
      Ms. Okafor: What did you mean when you texted “I messed up big”?
      Jordan: “I broke the two-person rule by taking the box alone. I knew I’d be in trouble.”

      Investigation board: find the conflicts

      Strong jurors notice when two statements can’t both be true. Select two statements that conflict. There are four conflicting pairs hidden in this board. Some statements don’t conflict with anything.

      Debrief

      Here’s how your two trials turned out and how your thinking scored along the way.

      What changed between the courtrooms

      Highlighted rows show rules that are the same in both kinds of court. Not everything is different.

      Criminal caseCivil case
      Who brings itThe government (prosecutor)The person or group harmed (plaintiff)
      How sure the jury must beBeyond a reasonable doubt, on every elementMore likely than not (preponderance of the evidence)
      Ways to winProve every element of the crimeProve every part of any one claim. Here, carelessness was enough.
      Evidence from an illegal police searchUsually thrown out (Fourth Amendment)Usually allowed
      Must the defendant testify?No. The jury can’t hold silence against them (Fifth Amendment).The other side can call the defendant as a witness.
      HearsayUsually not allowed in either court. Witnesses should tell what they saw themselves, so they can be cross-examined.
      Cross-examinationBoth sides can question every witness in both courts.
      Jury voteMust be unanimousIn many states, not all jurors must agree
      Possible resultJail, fines, probation, paying money backPaying damages
      Can both happen?Yes. Double jeopardy only stops the government from trying someone twice for the same crime. A civil lawsuit is not a second criminal trial.

      Discussion questions

      1. The envelope could have changed the criminal jury’s mind, but they never saw it. Is it fair to throw out evidence that might be true? What would happen if police could search anywhere without a warrant?
      2. Jordan changed his story about locking the car. Does one changed detail mean a person is lying about everything?
      3. Taylor might be biased. Should a biased witness be ignored, or just weighed more carefully? What’s the difference?
      4. If Jordan didn’t steal but was careless, is it fair that he has to pay? Who should carry the loss: the treasurer who broke the rules, or the league?
